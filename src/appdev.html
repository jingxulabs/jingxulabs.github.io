APP Development

Improve human communication by humanizing AI-generated text

Most AI humanizers try to change how text looks. HumanizedAI tries to measure why it is tiring to read, rewrite those measured properties, and prove that the rewrite did not alter what the document says.

1Repository

github.com/jingxulabs/HumanizedAI

Python, MIT licensed. The calibration ships prebuilt, so there is nothing to train before a first run. A FastAPI web interface and a command line covering score, signals, features, run, serve and calibrate. The rewriter and the judge run on different vendors' models by design.

2Project overview and summary

Problem. Machine-written prose wears people out. Everything sounds the same, nothing surprises, and attention goes. Communication nobody attends to has lost its purpose. So the target is readability, not disguise: the tool ships no detector, never optimizes against one, and where reading well and looking less machine-made pull apart it follows readability. It also won't make anything true, since a faithful rewrite of a wrong original stays wrong.

Approach. 31 features are calibrated, each scored by how cleanly it separates a labelled human corpus from an AI one. 20 of them separate the two well enough to be picked as steering signals. On a given document a steering signal only becomes an instruction if it clears three filters (separation ≥ 0.65, normalized gap ≥ 0.15, and agreement on direction), and at most six are issued per rewrite, strongest discriminator first. The rewriter then builds a ledger of the assertions in the source and writes fresh prose from that ledger rather than editing sentences: the ledger is fixed, the prose is free. Seven deterministic gates can veto any candidate; an LLM judge running on a different vendor's model advises but cannot.

Result. The human score of AI-generated text improves significantly after a pass through the tool. The shallow-rewrite failure was the finding that mattered. Across 22 saved candidates the earlier pipeline returned text 88–96% word-identical to its input, with verbatim spans up to 276 words, and not one gate ever rejected it. After the ledger change, on the same document and model: word similarity 0.90 → 0.61, depth 0.14 → 0.58, surviving sentences 74% → 19%, longest copied span 89 → 27 words, with literals and meaning still passing. Widening the brief beyond the ten-feature starter set took execution from 27% to 59%.

3The twenty steering signals

Calibration runs over 248 human-written documents and 220 AI-written ones, computing all 31 features across both. The two corpora do different jobs. The human corpus defines the target range: for each feature the calibration stores human percentiles (p10, p25, p50, p75, p90). The AI corpus never defines that range. It establishes only how sharply a feature discriminates between the two populations.

Of those 31 features, 20 separate human from machine writing well enough to steer a rewrite. Separation is an AUC-like measure: 0.50 means no useful discrimination, 1.00 means perfect separation. The system follows the calibrated direction rather than intuition.

Gates

A gate is a pass or fail check that can reject a candidate rewrite outright. Gates veto; the score only ranks whatever survived them. Four properties define one in this codebase.

  1. It vetoes, it does not rank. Gates run before ranking. A candidate that fails one is discarded, no matter how well it scores.
  2. It tests an invariant, not a target. Violating it means the rewrite is wrong, a dropped figure or a third of the length gone, not merely less good. A missed style target ranks a candidate instead of discarding it.
  3. It is deterministic and cheap. No LLM calls, so every candidate gets every gate.
  4. It has a tier. literals and meaning are integrity gates: they protect content a reader cannot check by eye. The rest protect shape. When the pipeline has to ship the least-bad candidate, integrity wins.

6Competitor analysis and next step

Measured against the humanizer tools already on GitHub, HumanizedAI is one of the more technically ambitious designs in the category, particularly around verification and semantic integrity. I do not see many that match its combination of corpus-derived steering, enforced rewrite depth, deterministic integrity gates and a semantic audit.

The large existing projects still beat it today on adoption and convenience. The gap is not the method. It is everything around the method, and that is the work:

  • Maturity. Several thresholds were tuned while watching a single document fail, the control run over human fixtures is still outstanding, and negation remains invisible to the meaning gate at 0.995.
  • Installation. Too much setup stands between a curious reader and a first run.
  • English only. The feature set and the calibration both assume English.
  • Comparative evaluation. No broad head-to-head against the tools it claims to differ from, which is exactly the claim that most needs evidence.
  • Distribution and interface. The incumbents win on both, and adoption turns on those rather than on method.

Cabinet, your personal decision intelligence

A private council for your life. Consequential decisions fail in ways a chat window makes worse: one confident voice where there should be several, and no record of what you claimed. Cabinet seats 51 advisers across 10 tables, and then scores you against your own confidence. The model is interchangeable. The record is not.

1Build

React and FastAPI, with three model providers behind one interface. A fully functional offline mode means the product demonstrates with no credentials and no network, so nothing here depends on a key being live.

2The problem

Problem. Consequential decisions fail in three ways that conversational AI makes worse, not better. One confident voice where there should be several. No memory of what you claimed, so you never learn whether your 80% sure means anything. And an answer to the question as posed rather than the one you are avoiding.

Position. Cabinet treats this as a structural problem, not a model problem. Every part of the system that matters is the part a better model would not fix.

3The design: a council that disagrees with itself

A standing council, seated across ten tables by domain. 51 seats, and each bench is deliberately composed to disagree with itself. A council that nods is a chat window in costume.

The ten tables: Personal, Finance, Career, Education, AI, Risk, Health, Mind, Family, Leadership.

The round. Pose, fan out, synthesise, push back, decide, review. Five seats answer the same question concurrently and in isolation; none sees another's answer. A synthesis then names the agreement, the real fault line beneath the disagreement, what nobody raised, and what you should resolve before deciding. You can press one adviser privately, put it to the whole table, or re-run the round.

4Demo

Two screens from the running application. The first is the Leadership table being convened; the second is a round in progress, with each seat answering under its own badge.

5The feedback loop, which is the actual product

Every decision is recorded with a verdict, a confidence number, and a date to revisit it. When that date arrives you score the outcome, and the number moves.

Two rules are enforced in the system, not left to good intentions:

  • Confidence is sealed. Once an outcome is recorded, the confidence you claimed cannot be amended.
  • Entries are struck, not edited. An entry can be struck off whole, never quietly revised.

Everything on the intelligence view is computed from the record, not asked of a model. A model can tell you what to do; only your own history can tell you how much to trust yourself when you say seventy per cent.

6AI and human seats, together

Every seat is AI-answered; what differs is whether a real person stands behind it. Simulated figures are badged as simulated wherever they speak. Seats representing people in your life answer immediately as an explicitly labelled stand-in, and are superseded the moment the real person replies, by private link or by you recording what they told you. A third of the council sitting silent is worse than an attributed guess.

This is the bridge: the council is useful on day one with nobody else's participation, and becomes more truthful as they join.

7Position, and what is weak

Four things already do part of this job. The column that separates them is the last one, which is in each case a limit of structure rather than of effort or model quality.

AlternativeStrengthStructural limit
General AI assistant Breadth, zero setup One voice; nothing recorded; no calibration
Persona prompt packs Instant personas Personas in one context converge; no track record
Multi-agent frameworks Genuine parallelism Built for task completion, not for your judgement
Decision journals An honest record No counsel at all; high friction, so abandoned
Cabinet (this work) Counsel and record together The intersection. Counsel of the first, honesty of the last

The defensible asset is the record. Any competitor can seat a better model tomorrow; nobody can hand you two years of your own decisions with the confidence you claimed and the outcomes scored against it. That is switching cost, and unlike a model it compounds.

Three strengths are structural rather than asserted: genuine independence between advisers; calibration of the human rather than the model; and integrity defended at the system boundary.

Weaknesses, stated plainly:

  • Single-user. Without authentication yet.
  • The loop is slow by nature. The twentieth entry is where it begins telling you things, so retention through the cold period is the central product risk.
  • Outcomes are self-reported. The scoring has no referee but you.
  • Cost. The default gateway supports no prompt caching, putting cost per round at roughly 5× the design intent.
8Next steps
  • Correctness. Resolve one intermittent rendering defect in the synthesis view, and introduce schema migrations before the first change that must preserve real decisions.
  • Highest leverage. Per-domain calibration. You are well calibrated on Finance and badly overconfident on Career is the most valuable sentence this product could say, and the data to say it is already being recorded.
  • Adoption. Route questions to the right table automatically, and drive reviews from outside the app. The loop dies if reviews do not happen.
  • Scale. A shared event bus to permit more than one worker, authentication and multi-user, a cost model for the default provider, and an export path. The record is the user's most valuable asset and currently has no way out.

The Game of Life, Played by Two Models: KEV-4B Against GPT-5.2

Conway's Game of Life has no player. This one has two. Two colonies share a mirrored board, and each generation a decision model reads a report of its colony's position and gives one order — which the engine turns into thresholds and applies to four hundred cells at once. Azure is kev-4b on a laptop, Crimson is gpt-5.2 through a gateway. Same board, same report, same questions, same engine; the only variable is who decides. Ten matches went 9–1 to gpt-5.2; the same ten seeds, re-run after two changes to what the models read, went 8–2 the other way. Both are smoke tests and neither is a verdict — and the interesting part was never the scoreline but its cause: one word each model never said.

1Repository

Local, not yet published. SurvivalOfTheSmartest

TypeScript throughout — React 18, Vite, Tailwind and Recharts. Three processes run it: the local KEV server, a small Python proxy that puts KEV and the gateway behind one endpoint and one answer shape, and the game. The browser never holds a credential and never learns which vendor answered.

What runs with no model and no network. npm test — 40 tests across 5 files in about 9 seconds: geometry, simultaneity, reproducibility, fairness and balance. The simulation is deterministic, so the same seed and the same decisions replay exactly. Without that, two strategists cannot be compared at all.

2Project overview and summary

Conway's Game of Life, in one paragraph. A grid. Each square is alive or dead. The next frame is decided entirely by how many of a square's eight neighbours are alive: a dead square with exactly 3 live neighbours comes to life, a live square with 2 or 3 survives, anything else dies of loneliness or crowding. That is the whole specification, and it is famous because gliders, oscillators and self-replicating machines all fall out of it unbidden. It is a zero-player game: you set the first frame and watch.

What this adds, and why. Four things, each of which turns a demonstration into a contest.

  • A second colony. Two populations share one board and compete for the same squares. Crowding and loneliness still count your own kind only, so the enemy can neither suffocate you nor keep you company — it can only overwhelm you, which is a rule of its own.
  • Energy, terrain and movement. Cells hold energy, spend it to move and to breed, starve at zero, forage toward nutrients and die on hazards. Reproduction stops being free, which is what makes it a decision instead of an inevitability.
  • A player — one per colony. Each generation a decision model issues a single order. The order does not move a cell; it moves the thresholds the rules are evaluated with. Conway's rules are fixed forever. These bend every generation, according to a judgement.
  • Mutations. hardy, thrifty, fertile and swift each bend exactly one rule for the cell carrying them, so a trait can be watched paying off or not.

So Conway's Life asks whether complexity can come from nothing. This asks something narrower and more testable: does a model's judgement survive contact with an emergent system — one where the consequence of an order arrives two generations after the order does.

3The rules, in pictures

Six rules decide every square, and they are shown live beside the board because the doctrine moves their numbers each generation. Blue is your colony, ochre the other, and the marked square is the one the rule is about. These are the defaults, with no order in force.

One order, four hundred fates. A doctrine is not a move. The engine turns it into thresholds — birth neighbours, crowding limit, energy reserve, birth cost, the energy a newborn starts with, how hard to contest a square — and then applies those to every cell on the board. That indirection is the whole design: the model never touches a cell, so what is being measured is judgement about a population, not micromanagement of it.

And the trap the game is built around. EXPAND breeds into every loose square, including two-neighbour ones. It is locally sensible every single time, and it is how colonies die: packing cells together pushes their neighbour counts past the crowding limit, and every birth is paid out of the parents' energy, so a colony can breed itself into poverty. The collapse arrives two generations after the order that caused it, which is exactly the lag a greedy decision-maker cannot see. The cascade panel traces it back — gen 6: 6 died → gen 7: 5 died → gen 9: 46 died — and names the order that started it.

Balance is a test, not a taste. If one order simply wins, the model is not deciding anything and the comparison measures nothing. So EXPAND's three counterweights — crowding limit, newborn energy, birth cost — are swept by balance.test.ts, which fails unless the strongest order stays at or below 75% of matches while fewer than a quarter of boards go extinct. At the setting it picks, EXPAND sits exactly on that ceiling at 0.75, ahead of FORAGE 0.63, CONSOLIDATE 0.56, DEFEND 0.31 and RETREAT 0.16. Before the retune, the strongest order won 100% of them.

4The design: what each model is given, and what it returns

One request per colony per generation. Both sides receive the same report format and the same four questions, so any difference in play is a difference in judgement. The report is the entire input: no model sees the board, the history, or what the other model was asked.

In — the report

Position
Population, mean energy, births and deaths last generation with their causes, open ground adjacent, nutrients and hazards in reach.
Pressure
The same figures for the other colony, and where the two are in contact.
Finished counts, never conclusions
A line reads “9 cells have 4+ neighbours and will suffocate next generation”. The arithmetic is the engine's; the inference is the model's. That split is what makes a 4B model viable here — it never has to count anything.

Out — four typed answers

EXPAND · CONSOLIDATE · DEFEND · FORAGE · RETREAT — with a probability over all five, not just the winner.

The asymmetry worth naming. KEV answers all four against a single prefill of the report and returns a calibrated probability from a trained pointer head — so on an ambiguous board it says CONSOLIDATE at 0.11 and the game shows the 0.11. GPT-5.2 receives the same report as a prompt and writes the four answers as JSON, including a confidence it chose. Both are parsed into the same structure, so the engine cannot tell them apart.

Fairness, enforced rather than intended. The map is mirrored left-to-right in terrain and starting cells; sides swap every match; births resolve simultaneously. That last one was a real bug — births were read from the array as it was written, so low-index cells bred first and compounded, and two colonies given identical orders finished 155 to 77. They now finish 120 to 109, and a test fails if the bias returns.

And nothing is faked when an endpoint is down. If a model is unreachable the round falls back to a local heuristic, the card reads endpoint down — not this model, the readout turns amber, and the scorecard counts it under fallbacks used. A silent fallback would turn the whole comparison into a measurement of the heuristic.

What KEV's probability is a probability of

The number beside each order is not a word the model wrote down. For a choice question with K options, a pointer head scores each supplied option against the state and the answer is a distribution over exactly those options:

p(aq = k | S, q) = exp(zk / T) Σj=1..K exp(zj / T) — a softmax over the options, at the fitted temperature
zk = (Wk hk) · (Wq hd) / √dp — the pointer score: option k read against the decision point
confidence = pmax − 1/K 1 − 1/K — distance above chance: 0 at a flat split, 1 at certainty
S
the state — this colony's report for this generation, prefilled once and shared by all four questions
q
the question: its instructions, and the K option names with their written criteria
hd, hk
the hidden states at the decision point of q's branch, and at option k
dp
the pointer dimension, 256; the 1/√dp scale keeps the dot product in range as the head widens
T
one calibration temperature, 2.41 for this checkpoint, fitted on held-out rows and applied at inference only — training always sees T = 1, and dividing by a scalar cannot change which option wins

Which is the finding, not the illustration. KEV chose FORAGE — and it beat EXPAND by 0.017 and RETREAT by 0.026. Chance on five options is 0.200; the winner polled 0.254. This is not a model that knows what to do and reports it modestly. It is a model telling you, accurately, that it can barely tell these five orders apart on this board. Over 15 boards sampled the same way the mean winning probability is 0.290, the mean gap over second place is 0.037, and 14 of 15 come back under 0.15 confidence.

And that is what the temperature is for. The same scores at T = 1 give FORAGE 0.321 and a confidence of 0.152 — the identical order, chosen for the identical reason, reported as more than twice as sure. T = 2.41 is the value that made these numbers mean what they say on held-out boards, and what they say here is not much. Which option wins never depended on it; how sure the game is allowed to look entirely did.

Read fold 6 with this in hand. A decision settled by 0.017 is one that any small change to the report can flip — and the tournament results turn out to hinge on exactly that.

Now the part that matters for the game: what it is not conditioned on. The four questions share the prefill S and are isolated from one another — each branch attends to the state and to its own options, never to another question or another answer. So a turn produces four separate conditionals p(aq | S, q), not a joint p(doctrine, aggression, risk, abandon | S). The model cannot make its aggression answer depend on the doctrine it just chose, because when it answers it has not chosen one.

That is a real constraint and it is deliberate. It is what lets four questions ride on one prefill — the reason a 4B model answers a turn in 2.5s instead of four round trips — and it is why composition is the engine's job: the doctrine picks the threshold set, and aggression and risk then modulate it. A model that could condition each answer on the last would be strictly more expressive and strictly slower, and it would also make the four answers impossible to attribute separately when a colony collapses.

GPT-5.2, by contrast, writes one JSON object. Its four fields are a joint sample — later fields are conditioned on the tokens of the earlier ones — and its confidence is a number it chose to emit rather than a function of anything. The two columns in the scorecard labelled confidence are therefore different quantities with the same name, which is worth remembering before comparing them: the separation between easy and hard boards is comparable, the absolute values are not.

6Two ten-match runs that disagree, and the one word behind both

Read this as a smoke test. Ten matches of twenty generations, one board size, one prompt, one family of seeds, both models live throughout — 200 decisions a side. That is enough to prove the harness runs end to end and to show where two strategists diverge. It is not enough to rank them, and nothing below should be read as a measurement of either model. At the generation limit the engine declares the larger colony the winner — breaking an exact tie on territory, then on stored energy — and that scoreboard turns out to matter more than anything either model did.

It has now been run twice, and the two runs disagree. The first went 9–1 to gpt-5.2, an exact two-sided binomial p of 0.021 against a coin. The same ten seeds, re-run after two changes to the document the models read, went 8–2 to kev-4b — p of 0.109, which clears no bar at all. Both models were live throughout both runs, with no illegal answers and no fallbacks in 800 decisions. I am leaving both on the page. A smoke test that reverses when you touch the prompt has told you something true about the sample size, and deleting the inconvenient half of it would be the one move that makes the page dishonest.

It is not the board. Sides swapped each match, and GPT won from both: four times as Crimson, five as Azure. It is not reliability either — neither model produced a single illegal answer or needed a single fallback in 400 live decisions.

It is one word. Both models reached for FORAGE almost identically — 140 times against 139 out of 200. The entire difference is the second choice. KEV played RETREAT 58 times and CONSOLIDATE never. GPT-5.2 played CONSOLIDATE 61 times and RETREAT never.

And the re-run appeared to confirm it. KEV's RETREAT count fell from 58 of 200 decisions to 11; its FORAGE count rose to 188. GPT-5.2 barely moved. The order the first analysis blamed for the 9–1 went away, and so did the 9–1. It is a tidy story and I believed it for about an hour. It is wrong, and the next four paragraphs are how that came apart — which is the most useful thing on this page.

Why KEV answered differently

KEV is deterministic: the same board and the same report return the same order, the same probability and the same confidence, to four decimal places, on repeated calls. So a different answer in the second run has to mean a different input. There were only two candidate inputs — the report-builder fix and the win-condition block — so both were put back and taken away in all four combinations, and the same 15 boards were put to the checkpoint under each.

The RETREAT column is flat. One or two of fifteen under every variant, and a mean probability that never leaves the neighbourhood of chance. On matched boards the report change does not make KEV retreat less. The sample was if anything stacked the other way: all 15 of those boards carry an exposed group, against 10 to 12 for boards the two policies actually produce. The stance was never the channel.

The two score answers are not flat. Aggression rises 0.170 and risk falls 0.089, on the same boards, in the same direction, every time. Aggression is not advice — the engine reads it as contestWeight = 0.7 + 0.3 × aggression, so KEV entered every contested square 4.4% stronger for the whole of the second run. A contested square is one both colonies qualify to breed into; winning more of them yields more cells, which yields more frontage, which yields more contested squares. Twenty generations of that is not a small effect, and it does not appear anywhere in a stance tally.

Replaying one match settles it. Seed 1000 flipped between the runs. Played again under the first run's report it goes to GPT-5.2, 64–79; under the second run's report it goes to KEV, 67–55. Both reproduce what the tournaments recorded. The two games diverge at generation 1 — not on the order, which is FORAGE both times, but on risk and aggression. Across that whole match KEV played RETREAT seven times under the old report and six under the new. Nearly identical, and the result still inverted.

The ablation was an accident. The first attempt at that replay had a bug: it pinned aggression and risk to 1 instead of passing the model's answers through. GPT-5.2 won under both reports. Disable the channel and the reversal disappears, which is the control nobody would have thought to run on purpose.

Why the question moves at all. It asks how hard to contest squares the other colony also wants. The second run's report is the first one that tells KEV it is in a race scored on population, where it stands on three measures, and how many generations are left. Scoring "contest harder" higher in a countdown is an unremarkable response. The surprise is the size of what it bought — and that the visible word, the one the interface prints in 21-point type under the arena, was the wrong place to look the entire time. The benchmark was moved by a sub-answer nobody was reading.

What is still owed. The aggression shift is measured on 15 boards and reproduced inside one match. The causal link to the tournament is demonstrated on 1 of the 10 seeds. The other six that flipped have not been replayed, and until they are this is a mechanism with one worked instance rather than a result.

And that word is expensive on this scoreboard. RETREAT raises the energy a cell keeps back before breeding to 5 — the highest of any doctrine — and cuts contest weight to 0.6, so a retreating colony both breeds less and yields contested ground. CONSOLIDATE holds the reserve at 4 and keeps breeding on exactly three. Sixty-odd calls of that difference, spread across ten matches, is a third more cells at the bell: 84.6 against 64.8.

Which is not the same as playing worse. KEV's surviving cells finish with more energy each — 3.48 against 3.23. It is playing not to die, in a game scored by headcount at generation 20 — the later tiebreaks only bite on an exact tie, so headcount is what it is playing against. Change the win condition to “still alive at generation 50” or “holds the nutrient squares” and this table is not obviously the same table. The honest headline is that GPT-5.2 is better at this scoreboard, and that a scoreboard is a choice someone made.

What changed between the two runs. Two things, which is the problem with attributing the reversal to either. The first is a fix to the report builder, which had been handing both models a line reading Deaths 0 (5 overcrowded, 1 starved) — a count that contradicted its own breakdown, in the one document either model is given to reason over. The second is that the win condition got written down: the criteria now live in one module the engine settles matches with, the report states them to both models along with the live standing on all three measures, and the interface shows them beside the board. Two edits, one sample of ten, confounded by construction. Separating them means re-running the same seeds with the win-condition block taken back out, and that has not been done.

What would turn the smoke test into a result. Vary the board size and the generation limit, run the same seeds under a second prompt, and push the match count far enough that a stance profile and not a scoreline is the unit of measurement. Ten matches was never going to be it; two ten-match runs pointing opposite ways is just the clearest available proof of that, and it cost two reversals to get.

One run is not on this page, and that is the point of saying so. A third ten-match run finished 5–5 and is void: 54 of GPT-5.2's 200 decisions were the local heuristic rather than the model, after the gateway failed under sustained load. The script counted the fallbacks and printed the scoreline anyway. It now records why each one happened and states that the run is not a measurement of that model — because a 27% fallback rate reported as a scoreline is the exact failure this harness was built to make impossible, and it got within one careless paragraph of happening here.

7Why this is interesting: evolution, and strategy

On evolution: fitness is not fertility. The board makes a selection argument visible in about forty seconds. A colony that breeds at every opportunity packs itself past the crowding limit and spends the energy its next generation needed — so the most fertile strategy reliably produces the smallest population two generations later. Energy is the budget constraint that turns reproduction into an economic decision rather than a free action, and the mutations bend exactly one rule each, so a trait can be watched earning its keep. None of that is modelled; it falls out of six local rules, which is Conway's original point put to work.

On strategy: this is what a policy is. One order applies to four hundred cells, none of which the decider can see individually. That is the shape of every real strategic choice — a hiring freeze, a pricing rule, a triage protocol — and it has the same failure mode: the locally sensible order is the one that kills you, and the bill arrives two moves after the decision, attached to something that looks like a different problem. The cascade panel exists to re-attach it.

And on evaluating models. A static benchmark asks a model one question and grades the answer. It cannot ask whether a judgement compounds, because nothing in a fixed test answers back. A world does: here the same decision is right on generation 3 and fatal on generation 11, and no amount of per-question accuracy predicts which. That is the case for environments over benchmarks, and it is cheap to make — the engine is a few hundred lines, and the thing being tested is a 4B model on a laptop.

The result that stayed with me. Two models with very different budgets played the same board and separated on a single stance neither of them ever crossed into: one never retreats, the other never consolidates. Not a difference of capability, and not visible in any accuracy column — a difference in temperament, which only showed up because something was keeping score over time.
