Gauss, Ceres, and what a prediction is worth.

Overview

Gauss, 1805. Legendre published the method first, in 1805; Gauss claimed he had been using it since 1795. They argued about it for years.

Ceres was recovered on 31 December 1801, within half a degree of the predicted position.

Three sightings, six orbital elements. The problem was overdetermined in the wrong direction, which is why it needed a method and not just arithmetic.

In 1805 the asteroid Ceres was observed three times and then lost behind the sun. To recover its orbit from three noisy sightings, Carl Friedrich Gauss formalized the method of least squares.

Every loss function since is a descendant of that problem. Something real is out there; the measurements are few and corrupted; the best available answer is the estimate that disagrees least with what you actually saw. Not truth, but the residual you can live with.

I build systems on that premise. The interesting question about a model has never been what it predicts. It is what the prediction is worth, and how anyone downstream would know when it stops being worth that.
