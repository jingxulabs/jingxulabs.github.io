Gauss, 1805. Legendre published the method first, in 1805; Gauss claimed he had been using it since 1795. They argued about it for years.

Ceres was recovered on 31 December 1801, within half a degree of the predicted position.

In 1805 the asteroid Ceres was observed three times and then lost behind the sun. To recover its orbit from three noisy sightings, Carl Friedrich Gauss formalized the method of least squares.

Every loss function since is a descendant of that problem. Something real is out there; the measurements are few and corrupted; the best available answer is the estimate that disagrees least with what you actually saw. Not truth, but the residual you can live with.

I build systems on that premise. The interesting question about a model has never been what it predicts. It is what the prediction is worth, and how anyone downstream would know when it stops being worth that.

Three of the directives run against conventional writing advice. Adding specificity and concrete examples is backwards, because the AI corpus already carries more of both. And humans vary sentence length far more than sentence construction, so "vary your structure" turns out to be the wrong instruction.

Negation is invisible to the meaning gate. Flipping a sentence's polarity still scores 0.995, because the embeddings don't encode it at this granularity. It's pinned in the tests so it can't be mistaken for coverage.

Several thresholds were tuned while watching one document fail. A control run across human fixtures is still outstanding.

Humanizing AI-generated text

github.com/jingxulabs/HumanizedAI

Most AI humanizers try to change how text looks. HumanizedAI tries to measure why it is tiring to read, rewrite those measured properties, and prove that the rewrite did not alter what the document says.

Problem. Machine-written prose wears people out. Everything sounds the same, nothing surprises, and attention goes. Communication nobody attends to has lost its purpose. So the target is readability, not disguise: the tool ships no detector, never optimizes against one, and where reading well and looking less machine-made pull apart it follows readability. It also won't make anything true, since a faithful rewrite of a wrong original stays wrong.

Approach. More than 30 signals are calibrated, each scored by how cleanly it separates a labelled human corpus from an AI one. A signal only becomes an instruction if it clears three filters (separation ≥ 0.65, normalized gap ≥ 0.15, and agreement on direction), and at most six are issued per rewrite, strongest discriminator first. The rewriter then builds a ledger of the assertions in the source and writes fresh prose from that ledger rather than editing sentences: the ledger is fixed, the prose is free. Seven deterministic gates can veto any candidate; an LLM judge running on a different vendor's model advises but cannot.

Result. The shallow-rewrite failure was the finding that mattered. Across 22 saved candidates the earlier pipeline returned text 88–96% word-identical to its input, with verbatim spans up to 276 words, and not one gate ever rejected it. After the ledger change, on the same document and model: word similarity 0.90 → 0.61, depth 0.14 → 0.58, surviving sentences 74% → 19%, longest copied span 89 → 27 words, with literals and meaning still passing. Widening the brief beyond the ten-feature starter set took execution from 27% to 59%.
