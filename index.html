Overview

Gauss, 1805. Legendre published the method first, in 1805; Gauss claimed he had been using it since 1795. They argued about it for years.

Ceres was recovered on 31 December 1801, within half a degree of the predicted position.

In 1805 the asteroid Ceres was observed three times and then lost behind the sun. To recover its orbit from three noisy sightings, Carl Friedrich Gauss formalized the method of least squares.

Every loss function since is a descendant of that problem. Something real is out there; the measurements are few and corrupted; the best available answer is the estimate that disagrees least with what you actually saw. Not truth, but the residual you can live with.

I build systems on that premise. The interesting question about a model has never been what it predicts. It is what the prediction is worth, and how anyone downstream would know when it stops being worth that.

Experimentations

Improve human communication by humanizing AI-generated text

Most AI humanizers try to change how text looks. HumanizedAI tries to measure why it is tiring to read, rewrite those measured properties, and prove that the rewrite did not alter what the document says.

1Repository

github.com/jingxulabs/HumanizedAI

Python, MIT licensed. The calibration ships prebuilt, so there is nothing to train before a first run. A FastAPI web interface and a command line covering score, signals, features, run, serve and calibrate. The rewriter and the judge run on different vendors' models by design.

2Project overview and summary

Problem. Machine-written prose wears people out. Everything sounds the same, nothing surprises, and attention goes. Communication nobody attends to has lost its purpose. So the target is readability, not disguise: the tool ships no detector, never optimizes against one, and where reading well and looking less machine-made pull apart it follows readability. It also won't make anything true, since a faithful rewrite of a wrong original stays wrong.

Approach. 31 features are calibrated, each scored by how cleanly it separates a labelled human corpus from an AI one. 20 of them separate the two well enough to be picked as steering signals. On a given document a steering signal only becomes an instruction if it clears three filters (separation ≥ 0.65, normalized gap ≥ 0.15, and agreement on direction), and at most six are issued per rewrite, strongest discriminator first. The rewriter then builds a ledger of the assertions in the source and writes fresh prose from that ledger rather than editing sentences: the ledger is fixed, the prose is free. Seven deterministic gates can veto any candidate; an LLM judge running on a different vendor's model advises but cannot.

Result. The human score of AI-generated text improves significantly after a pass through the tool. The shallow-rewrite failure was the finding that mattered. Across 22 saved candidates the earlier pipeline returned text 88–96% word-identical to its input, with verbatim spans up to 276 words, and not one gate ever rejected it. After the ledger change, on the same document and model: word similarity 0.90 → 0.61, depth 0.14 → 0.58, surviving sentences 74% → 19%, longest copied span 89 → 27 words, with literals and meaning still passing. Widening the brief beyond the ten-feature starter set took execution from 27% to 59%.

3The twenty steering signals

Calibration runs over 248 human-written documents and 220 AI-written ones, computing all 31 features across both. The two corpora do different jobs. The human corpus defines the target range: for each feature the calibration stores human percentiles (p10, p25, p50, p75, p90). The AI corpus never defines that range. It establishes only how sharply a feature discriminates between the two populations.

Of those 31 features, 20 separate human from machine writing well enough to steer a rewrite. Separation is an AUC-like measure: 0.50 means no useful discrimination, 1.00 means perfect separation. The system follows the calibrated direction rather than intuition.

Gates

A gate is a pass or fail check that can reject a candidate rewrite outright. Gates veto; the score only ranks whatever survived them. Four properties define one in this codebase.

  1. It vetoes, it does not rank. Gates run before ranking. A candidate that fails one is discarded, no matter how well it scores.
  2. It tests an invariant, not a target. Violating it means the rewrite is wrong, a dropped figure or a third of the length gone, not merely less good. A missed style target ranks a candidate instead of discarding it.
  3. It is deterministic and cheap. No LLM calls, so every candidate gets every gate.
  4. It has a tier. literals and meaning are integrity gates: they protect content a reader cannot check by eye. The rest protect shape. When the pipeline has to ship the least-bad candidate, integrity wins.

6Competitor analysis and next step

Measured against the humanizer tools already on GitHub, HumanizedAI is one of the more technically ambitious designs in the category, particularly around verification and semantic integrity. I do not see many that match its combination of corpus-derived steering, enforced rewrite depth, deterministic integrity gates and a semantic audit.

The large existing projects still beat it today on adoption and convenience. The gap is not the method. It is everything around the method, and that is the work:

  • Maturity. Several thresholds were tuned while watching a single document fail, the control run over human fixtures is still outstanding, and negation remains invisible to the meaning gate at 0.995.
  • Installation. Too much setup stands between a curious reader and a first run.
  • English only. The feature set and the calibration both assume English.
  • Comparative evaluation. No broad head-to-head against the tools it claims to differ from, which is exactly the claim that most needs evidence.
  • Distribution and interface. The incumbents win on both, and adoption turns on those rather than on method.

Tool selection from user intent: which stage actually fails

Public benchmarks hand an agent ten candidate tools, so they can only measure choosing. Production agents face thousands, where the hard part is finding. ToolSelection builds the missing condition, 3,551 tools instead of ten, and finds 83% of errors in retrieval, not selection.

1Repository

github.com/jingxulabs/ToolSelection

Every measured figure in this section is read from the repository's data/eval files when you open it, not transcribed here.

2The question, and why benchmarks cannot ask it

Problem. An agent turning a request into a tool call does it in two stages. Published benchmarks present one to ten candidates, so retrieval is solved before the measurement starts and only selection gets scored. A production agent carries thousands. Nobody had measured that condition, so nobody knew which stage to fix.

  • retrieverUser intent against the whole catalog: 3,551 tools narrowed to roughly 100. Dense embedding search. The final retriever is the gte-large embedding model over v3 document text.
  • selectorShortlist to a single call: 100 candidates to 1. An LLM reading the candidates.

The two stages fail for unrelated reasons and are usually optimized as one.

3Data

Three sources pooled into one catalog of 3,551 tools: 199 carrying labels and 3,352 acting as distractors, a ratio of 17.8 to 1. The labels come from a GPT-4-built set, so they are independent of the model under test.

One number justifies the whole construction. At 199 candidates retrieval is solved and unmeasurable. At 3,551, 14% of correct tools never reach the shortlist, so no selector can recover them.

4Design

Three choices earned their keep:

  • Tools held out before intents. 39 of the 199 labeled tools are withheld entirely. Splitting intents at random instead would leak tool identity across the split.
  • A pre-registered stop rule. If retrieval misses exceeded 40% of errors, the model-training track was to be abandoned unbuilt. It tripped.
  • Self-reported ambiguity. The intent generator could mark a pair inseparable: identical function and parameters, differing only in HTTP verb. 10.1% did. The selector independently flagged 32.7% of those as tied, against 0.0% of the separable ones. Two mechanisms agreeing removed the need for human annotation.

The arms

A cumulative ladder: each arm inherits everything beneath it and is measured against the arm immediately beneath, so each row answers a marginal question.

ArmConditionMarginal question

1 Base model, catalog as written, all tools in context or truncated The true starting point.
2 + rewritten descriptions, parameters, negative examples How much of this is just bad documentation?
3 + embedding retrieval, top-k shortlist Does narrowing help, and at what k?
4 + SFT on intent → call pairs What does supervised fitting buy?
5 + GRPO over shortlists What does RL add beyond SFT?
5-alt + DPO on preference pairs Half the benefit for a tenth of the effort?

∞ Oracle retrieval: gold tool always in the shortlist The ceiling. Isolates selection and grounding from retrieval.
∞∞ Human expert on a 200-item subsample Absolute ceiling, and an estimate of label noise.

The two oracle arms are not optional: without arm ∞ you cannot tell whether a failure is retrieval or selection, which is the diagnosis that decides whether arm 5 is worth building. It wasn't: the stop rule tripped and arms 4 and 5 were dropped.

One design choice inside arm 5. RL would have run over shortlists, not tool identities: the model discriminates among candidate documents in context, so tool identities never enter the weights. That preserves zero-shot generalization across catalog changes and makes arms 3 and 5 compose rather than compete.

Reading the two metrics

  • R@kRecall at k: the share of queries whose correct tool appears anywhere in the top k. A hit at rank 1 and a hit at rank 25 both count as one for R@25. It is the right measure when a later stage will read the whole shortlist.
  • MRRMean reciprocal rank: the average of 1/rank of the correct tool. Rank 1 scores 1.00, rank 2 scores 0.50, rank 10 scores 0.10, a miss scores 0. It rewards moving the right answer up the list, not just getting it in.

The difference is the whole story of the fusion row. Blending moved correct tools upward inside the shortlist, which MRR registers (+0.023, interval clear of zero), but it did not move enough of them all the way to rank 1 for R@1 to show a defensible gain (p = 0.124). A gain in MRR with none in R@1 means better ordering without a better top pick.

What those two middle rows are. They are the same experiment run twice on the identical cross-encoder scores, and they differ only in how those scores are combined with the retriever's. That is the whole point of the pair: the signal is held fixed and only the combination rule changes.

  • replacingInside the top 100, throw the dense ordering away and sort purely by cross-encoder score. This is what “reranking” usually means.
  • blendingKeep both. Each score is turned into a per-query z-score over the same window, then mixed at α = 0.2, so the reranker gets 20% of the say and the retriever keeps 80%.

scorefused = (1 − α) · z(dense) + α · z(cross-encoder)

Depth. Both rows are measured at depth 100: the retriever keeps 100 candidates and the cross-encoder rescores all of them. Every number in them is a mean over the five generated splits at that depth, so they are comparable to each other and to the dense baseline. Replacing is α = 1, which is why the two rows are endpoints of one sweep rather than two unrelated ideas. The same scores help at α = 0.2 and hurt at 1.

What “better encoder + richer document text” means

Two independent changes, bundled in the table above because they shipped together. Only one is about the model.

The encoder. all-MiniLM-L6-v2 → gte-large, which won on every split of a five-encoder sweep. Size is not the lever: bge-large ≈ bge-base, and e5-base-v2 lost to the much smaller MiniLM even with its asymmetric prefixes handled correctly.

Why the text helps. The gain lands where the losses were: on tools whose gold description runs to five words or fewer, R@10 goes 0.348 → 0.568. Two endpoints differing only in HTTP verb are identical documents under v0, and the method token is the whole of what separates them. The v3 text is worth a mean +6.0 points R@10 and lifts every encoder tried, positive in 24 of 25 encoder × split cells.

The path tokens are narrower than they look. humanize() strips {braces} before tokenizing, so they separate endpoints that add a literal segment (/admin/hooks against /admin/hooks/{hook_id}/pings) but not endpoints differing only by a path parameter. /timezone/{area}/{location} and …/{region} both reduce to timezone, and stay separated only by their names and parameter lists.

Methodological debt. The sweep ran before the dev/test partition was cut, so gte-large/v3 was chosen while looking at what is now test. The single test access should go to the composed pipeline, not to re-confirming the encoder.

6Five insights
  • The field is optimizing the stage that isn't broken. Benchmarks hand over ten candidates, so published work tunes selection. Before optimizing either stage, force the right answer into the shortlist and see whether accuracy jumps.
  • How you combine signals matters more than which signals you have. Identical cross-encoder scores: fused +9% R@1, substituted −15%. The tell is the depth curve: fusion improves with depth, replacement degrades. A signal at 0.8× your main signal's quality is worth keeping, not worth trusting alone.
  • But fusion has a floor: it reweights information, it cannot manufacture it. BM25 fused gave nothing: its top 100 held fewer correct tools than dense's, and almost none dense missed. The union added zero correct tools on 4 of 6 splits. Hence a cheap screening test: check whether dense ∪ new reaches errors dense alone cannot. It would have predicted both negative results.
  • Plausible mechanism plus catalog statistics is not evidence. Three interventions were justified that way and all three died against where the error mass is. 441 badly-documented tools sounds compelling, but they are the correct answer for only 3.6% of queries and 4.4% of failures; the 73% with a well-documented correct tool carry 66%. The subtler trap generalizes to any LLM-generated eval set: badly-documented tools appear as correct answers at 0.150× their catalog rate, because the generator had to read a description to write a request for it. The benchmark under-samples the very tools the fix would help.
  • Point estimates here are noise, and intervals are nearly free. Paired tests off cached scores changed the status of 2 of 4 claims, including the headline: fusion's +9.4% R@1 does not survive (p = 0.124), the MRR gain does.

Two paradoxes. Difficulty is not ordered: indirect requests are harder than distractor-context ones (R@10 0.564 against 0.605). And you cannot threshold for “no tool applies”: unanswerable queries score a higher top-1 similarity (0.479) than real ones (0.445).

The engineering bind: the only intervention that helped costs 5 s/query and cannot ship; the only one fast enough to ship (3 ms) does not help.

7How JEV System one help?

Short answer. It could help, but in selection, which this experiment shows is not the bottleneck. The name is a Kahneman reference: a System One model returns a typed decision plus probabilities rather than a generated response. That output shape is the reason the arm is worth testing, because a calibrated decision can be fused, thresholded and audited. That is the form the fusion result above says to keep as a signal rather than trust as a replacement.

Why it cannot help retrieval. A semantic decision layer reads the query and a candidate together. That joint reading is what makes it accurate and what rules it out as a first-stage ranker. It is bounded above by recall@K, because it only ever sees the shortlist: at K = 100 the ceiling is 0.861, so 14% of gold tools stay unreachable however good the judgment is. It can convert recall into precision; it cannot create recall. And its cost scales with candidates rather than with the index: 3.3–5.0 s over 100 candidates, against microseconds for precomputed vectors.

Measured, not assumed. The cross-encoder arm is a semantic decision layer in exactly this position, and used by replacement it was significantly harmful (R@1 0.253 → 0.214 at depth 100, p = 0.005).

The metric to add. Report end-to-end accuracy factored, not as one number. Only this form says which stage moved:

P(correct final) = P(gold in top-K) × P(select gold ∣ gold in top-K)

The left factor is retrieval, the right the selector's conditional accuracy. Retrieval carries about 83% of the error mass; the right factor is already about 0.93.

The real hypothesis. Not whether JEV System One selects better than the current selector, but whether a cheap semantic decision layer lets us widen retrieval enough to recover more gold tools while holding conditional accuracy high, buying back the left factor without paying it out of the right. recall@100 (0.861) sits far above recall@1 (0.259), and that gap is the prize.

Falsifiable by a K sweep: if conditional accuracy decays faster than recall climbs, widening K is a loss whatever the selector costs.
