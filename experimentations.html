Scaling Tool Selection: Retrieval Bottlenecks and a System One Proposal

Public benchmarks hand an agent ten candidate tools, so they can only measure choosing. Production agents face thousands, where the hard part is finding. ToolSelection builds the missing condition, 3,551 tools instead of ten, and finds 83% of errors in retrieval, not selection.

2The question, and why benchmarks cannot ask it

Problem. An agent turning a request into a tool call does it in two stages. Published benchmarks present one to ten candidates, so retrieval is solved before the measurement starts and only selection gets scored. A production agent carries thousands. Nobody had measured that condition, so nobody knew which stage to fix.

  • retrieverUser intent against the whole catalog: 3,551 tools narrowed to roughly 25. Dense embedding search. The final retriever is the gte-large embedding model over v3 document text.
  • selectorShortlist to a single call: 25 candidates to 1. A large language model (LLM) reading the candidates.
  • rerankerNot in this pipeline. A cross-encoder stage between the two was measured on its own over a depth-100 window and never composed in, so every number below is two-stage. See fold 5.

The two stages fail for unrelated reasons and are usually optimized as one.

What “roughly 25” rests on. It is a provisional choice, not a tuned one. The selector's 0.93 conditional accuracy was measured at k = 20 under oracle retrieval, with the correct tool forced into the shortlist, which is what isolates selection from retrieval and makes the factored metric in fold 7 computable. The sweep that would fix k properly has not been run.

4Design

Three choices earned their keep:

  • Tools held out before intents. 39 of the 199 labeled tools are withheld entirely. Splitting intents at random instead would leak tool identity across the split.
  • A pre-registered stop rule. If retrieval misses exceeded 40% of errors, the model-training track was to be abandoned unbuilt. It tripped.
  • Self-reported ambiguity. The intent generator could mark a pair inseparable: identical function and parameters, differing only in HTTP verb. 10.1% did. The selector independently flagged 32.7% of those as tied, against 0.0% of the separable ones. Two mechanisms agreeing removed the need for human annotation.

One design choice inside arm 5. RL would have run over shortlists, not tool identities: the model discriminates among candidate documents in context, so tool identities never enter the weights. That preserves zero-shot generalization across catalog changes and makes arms 3 and 5 compose rather than compete.

5Results

Six interventions, one of which worked. Effects carry paired tests, because in this regime point estimates are noise. Reading the two metrics

  • R@kRecall at k: the share of queries whose correct tool appears anywhere in the top k. A hit at rank 1 and a hit at rank 25 both count as one for R@25. It is the right measure when a later stage will read the whole shortlist.
  • MRRMean reciprocal rank: the average of 1/rank of the correct tool. Rank 1 scores 1.00, rank 2 scores 0.50, rank 10 scores 0.10, a miss scores 0. It rewards moving the right answer up the list, not just getting it in.

The difference is the whole story of the fusion row. Blending moved correct tools upward inside the shortlist, which MRR registers (+0.023, interval clear of zero), but it did not move enough of them all the way to rank 1 for R@1 to show a defensible gain (p = 0.124). A gain in MRR with none in R@1 means better ordering without a better top pick.

Encoder and cross-encoder

Two models, two jobs. The difference decides what can be precomputed, and therefore which one is allowed to look at the whole catalog.

Encoder, for retrieval

Here
bge-reranker-large

A query and one tool document, concatenated into a single input.

One relevance number for that one pair. No vector, nothing reusable.
Precomputable
No. How it reads the document depends on the query it was paired with, so there is no query-independent representation to store.

3.3–5.0 s over 100 candidates: one forward pass each.

That is the whole trade. Seeing both texts at once lets the cross-encoder's attention run between query tokens and document tokens, so it reads the document in light of what was actually asked. An encoder must commit each document to a fixed vector before it has ever seen the query. Giving that up is what buys the encoder its speed, and the speed is what makes 3,551 candidates affordable at all: the cross-encoder over the full catalog would be minutes per query. So the encoder narrows, the cross-encoder only ever reorders what the encoder already found, and its ceiling is whatever recall the encoder reached.

What those two middle rows are. Each candidate therefore carries two numbers, an encoder score and a cross-encoder score, and the two rows are the two ways of resolving them into one ordering. The signal is held fixed; only the combination rule changes. Blending z-scores each one first, which is what makes two unrelated scales addable:

scorefused = (1 − α) · z(dense) + α · z(cross-encoder)

α = 0.2 → 0.80 · z(dense) + 0.20 · z(cross-encoder)

α is the cross-encoder's weight, so the 20% sits on the cross-encoder and the dense score keeps 80%. The sweep confirms the reading at both ends: α = 0 reproduces the dense baseline exactly (R@1 0.253) and α = 1 reproduces the replacement row (0.214). The weaker signal is worth keeping at a minority weight and not worth trusting alone.

Depth, which is not the shortlist. Both rows are measured at depth 100: the cross-encoder rescores the retriever's top 100. That window is a rescoring budget, not the shortlist handed on to the selector, which stays at roughly 25. Reranking deeper than you intend to pass along is the normal arrangement, because reordering can only pull a tool up into the shortlist if it was scored in the first place. Every number in these rows is a mean over the five generated splits at depth 100, so they are comparable to each other and to the dense baseline. Replacing is α = 1, which is why the two rows are endpoints of one sweep rather than two unrelated ideas: the same scores help at α = 0.2 and hurt at 1.

Why the text helps. The gain lands where the losses were: on tools whose gold description runs to five words or fewer, R@10 goes 0.348 → 0.568. Two endpoints differing only in HTTP verb are identical documents under v0, and the method token is the whole of what separates them. The v3 text is worth a mean +6.0 points R@10 and lifts every encoder tried, positive in 24 of 25 encoder × split cells.

The path tokens are narrower than they look. humanize() strips {braces} before tokenizing, so they separate endpoints that add a literal segment (/admin/hooks against /admin/hooks/{hook_id}/pings) but not endpoints differing only by a path parameter. /timezone/{area}/{location} and …/{region} both reduce to timezone, and stay separated only by their names and parameter lists.

Methodological debt. The sweep ran before the dev/test partition was cut, so gte-large/v3 was chosen while looking at what is now test. The single test access should go to the composed pipeline, not to re-confirming the encoder.

6Five insights
  • The field is optimizing the stage that isn't broken. Benchmarks hand over ten candidates, so published work tunes selection. Before optimizing either stage, force the right answer into the shortlist and see whether accuracy jumps.
  • How you combine signals matters more than which signals you have. Identical cross-encoder scores: fused +9% R@1, substituted −15%. The tell is the depth curve: fusion improves with depth, replacement degrades. A signal at 0.8× your main signal's quality is worth keeping, not worth trusting alone.
  • But fusion has a floor: it reweights information, it cannot manufacture it. BM25 fused gave nothing: its top 100 held fewer correct tools than dense's, and almost none dense missed. The union added zero correct tools on 4 of 6 splits. Hence a cheap screening test: check whether dense ∪ new reaches errors dense alone cannot. It would have predicted both negative results.
  • Plausible mechanism plus catalog statistics is not evidence. Three interventions were justified that way and all three died against where the error mass is. 441 badly-documented tools sounds compelling, but they are the correct answer for only 3.6% of queries and 4.4% of failures; the 73% with a well-documented correct tool carry 66%. The subtler trap generalizes to any LLM-generated eval set: badly-documented tools appear as correct answers at 0.150× their catalog rate, because the generator had to read a description to write a request for it. The benchmark under-samples the very tools the fix would help.
  • Point estimates here are noise, and intervals are nearly free. Paired tests off cached scores changed the status of 2 of 4 claims, including the headline: fusion's +9.4% R@1 does not survive (p = 0.124), the MRR gain does.

Two paradoxes. Difficulty is not ordered: indirect requests are harder than distractor-context ones (R@10 0.564 against 0.605). And you cannot threshold for “no tool applies”: unanswerable queries score a higher top-1 similarity (0.479) than real ones (0.445).

The engineering bind: the only intervention that helped costs 5 s/query and cannot ship; the only one fast enough to ship (3 ms) does not help.

7How JEV System one help?

Short answer. It could help, but in selection, which this experiment shows is not the bottleneck. The name is a Kahneman reference: a System One model returns a typed decision plus probabilities rather than a generated response. That output shape is the reason the arm is worth testing, because a calibrated decision can be fused, thresholded and audited. That is the form the fusion result above says to keep as a signal rather than trust as a replacement.

Why it cannot help retrieval. A semantic decision layer reads the query and a candidate together. That joint reading is what makes it accurate and what rules it out as a first-stage ranker. It is bounded above by recall@K, because it only ever sees the shortlist: at K = 100 the ceiling is 0.861, so 14% of gold tools stay unreachable however good the judgment is. It can convert recall into precision; it cannot create recall. And its cost scales with candidates rather than with the index: 3.3–5.0 s over 100 candidates, against microseconds for precomputed vectors.

Measured, not assumed. The cross-encoder arm is a semantic decision layer in exactly this position, and used by replacement it was significantly harmful (R@1 0.253 → 0.214 at depth 100, p = 0.005).

The metric to add. Report end-to-end accuracy factored, not as one number. Only this form says which stage moved:

P(correct final) = P(gold in top-K) × P(select gold ∣ gold in top-K)

The left factor is retrieval, the right the selector's conditional accuracy. The headline decomposition composes them at K = 25: R@25 ≈ 0.65 against a conditional 0.93, giving end-to-end ≈ 0.60, of which about 83% of the error mass is retrieval. The 0.93 was measured at K = 20, and the sweep that would fix the operating point properly has not been run, so treat 25 as a provisional choice rather than a tuned one.

The real hypothesis. Not whether JEV System One selects better than the current selector, but whether a cheap semantic decision layer lets us widen retrieval enough to recover more gold tools while holding conditional accuracy high, buying back the left factor without paying it out of the right. recall@100 (0.861) sits far above recall@1 (0.259), and that gap is the prize.

Falsifiable by a K sweep: if conditional accuracy decays faster than recall climbs, widening K is a loss whatever the selector costs.

2The difference, exactly

One edge. Both architectures get the same model, the same two tools (search and lookup) and the same prompt vocabulary. The entire difference is one edge in the graph: the loop has an arrow that goes back. Everything people attribute to “agents” — retrying, re-querying, deciding it has enough, running too long — is a consequence of that arrow, and nothing else.

What that one edge changes

Fixed workflow (DAG — a directed acyclic graph: every edge goes forward)

Who chooses the next step
You do, at design time. The graph is written before the request arrives.
LLM calls per request
A constant. 2 here, every time, measured p95 — the 95th percentile, the value only one run in twenty exceeds — of 2, and max 2, over 100 runs.
Reacting to what it reads
It cannot. Both retrieval queries are written by the one decompose call, so hop 2 is composed before hop 1's result exists.
Retry on a failed tool
No edge for it. A 503 becomes a permanent hole in the synthesis context.
Characteristic failure
The quiet one: answering confidently from a paragraph it had no way to know was wrong. It always terminates — 100% clean here.
Cost distribution
Nearly a point mass. CV — the coefficient of variation, the spread of cost over its own mean, so a unitless number comparable between arms — is 0.31, and p95 only 1.8× the mean. You can put it in a capacity plan.

Agent loop (ReAct)

Who chooses the next step
The model does, at run time, having read what came back.
LLM calls per request
A random variable. Mean 3.05, p95 6, max 9 over the same 100 runs.
Reacting to what it reads
That is the mechanism. Each query is written after the previous observation, which is what makes an unknown number of hops reachable at all.
Retry on a failed tool
Free. A 503 is just another observation, and “try again” is already in its vocabulary.
Characteristic failure
The loud one: running to the step limit, or emitting an action the parser cannot read. 6% of runs here did not end cleanly.
Cost distribution
A long right tail. CV 0.81, p95 3.0× the mean. The mean is not what you provision for.

Two systems with the same mean cost are not the same system to operate. That is the line the accuracy table cannot show you, and it is the one that decides the architecture at 10M requests a day.

A third arm, so it is not a strawman. A hybrid — a classifier in front of a bounded loop — is what most production systems actually converge on, so it is measured alongside. It is also the only arm that can lose to both others, because a misroute means paying for the classification and then running the wrong plan.

The question is only two-hop on paper. Its own wording retrieves the gold paragraph, so there is no second hop for either arm to take — the loop's ability to write a query from what it just read had nothing to act on. The DAG's standing bet, that every question is a 2-hop bridge, costs nothing when the bet is irrelevant: it paid for one redundant retrieval and no decision. Most of HotpotQA's distractor split looks like this, which is why the workflow's EM in fold 7 is competitive at all, and why Case C had to be built from invented entities to find a task that separates the arms.

The loop was right on less evidence. It stopped after one search, having matched the haunting to a film of the same name, and never read the sentence naming the director. The workflow, which always fills four paragraph slots whether or not it needs them, had that sentence and did not use it. Cheaper and better evidenced — the opposite of the intuitive ordering, and the shape of the 2.2× the loop spends on clean 2-hop questions in fold 7.

6Design

The arms. Three, sharing one retriever, one prompt vocabulary and one model. Nothing in the tool layer knows which arm is calling it, so only control flow differs.

  • workflowdecompose → retrieve(hop1) → retrieve(hop2) → synthesize. Exactly 2 LLM calls.
  • reactThought → Action → Observation until finish[…], capped at max_steps. 1 to max_steps+1 calls.
  • hybridclassify → {single | comparison | bounded loop}. 2 to max_steps+2 calls. Present so the comparison is not a strawman.

The conditions are the experiment. Accuracy on clean questions teaches almost nothing — both architectures land in the same place. Each condition perturbs the environment in a way the two should answer differently, and each one's hypothesis is written down in conditions.py before the run, so the result is allowed to contradict it.

Fairness invariants

The comparison is worthless if these break, so they are enforced in the runner rather than left to convention.

  • Paired questions. Sampled once per (condition, seed) and reused across every arm, never resampled per arm. This is what makes the McNemar test valid and is why the traces in fold 4 are genuinely the same question twice.
  • Paired faults. Each tool environment is seeded from (question_id, seed), so an injected 503 lands on the same question for every arm.
  • Metered tokens, not estimated. The gateway's /openai/v1/ route is used specifically because it is the only one that returns a usage block.
  • A model-capability detail must never read as an architecture result. gpt-5.4-nano rejects stop with a 400. Only the loop sends one, so that single rejection would have zeroed the agent arm while the workflow arm ran fine. The client parses the rejected parameter out of the error, drops it and retries.

Dropping stop has a consequence the loop has to handle: without it the model can run on and write its own Observation: line. The loop truncates generations there, so it can never hallucinate evidence and “answer” without calling a tool.

8The forced case: variable-depth chains

HotpotQA is 2-hop by construction — the exact shape the workflow hardcodes — so it cannot test whether a loop does something a fixed graph structurally cannot. The chain task builds that case: successor chains of unknown depth, where resolving depth d needs d+1 retrievals and d is invisible from the question. Entity names are invented so no arm can shortcut through memorised knowledge, and 4 decoy chains make the end paragraphs indistinguishable, so blind guessing floors at about 20%. 9Five insights, and what would change the answer
  • Accuracy was the wrong column to look at. Three architectures, 15 paired comparisons, not one significant. Had the experiment reported EM alone it would have concluded “no difference” and been useless. The decision lives in cost variance: CV 0.31 against 0.81. One is a point mass you can put in an SLA, a service-level agreement; the other has a tail the model chooses at run time. The column was also generous: rescored for retrieved evidence, the loop falls from 0.510 to 0.357 while the workflow holds 0.480, because 15 of the loop's 50 correct answers were never retrieved at all.
  • Adaptivity is a tax until you can name the task that needs it. On clean 2-hop questions the loop spent 2.2× the tokens to rediscover a shape that was already written down. Case A in fold 4 shows the shape of that waste: a question the first retrieval already answered, where the loop's extra tokens bought no evidence the workflow did not already have.
  • But when the task needs it, nothing substitutes. 13–0, p = 0.0002. Two retrievals cannot traverse four links, and no prompt or model upgrade changes that. The useful question is never “loop or graph” in the abstract — it is whether the shape of the work is known before the request arrives.
  • Two of five pre-registered hypotheses were falsified, and that is the format working. Flaky tools were supposed to favour the loop and barely did; misleading retrieval was supposed to favour it and it did worse, because noticing a plausible wrong paragraph is a capability the model mostly lacks. Writing the hypothesis down before the run is what makes a falsification visible instead of quietly rewritten.
  • The biggest available win was in neither architecture. 39% of errors are the right entity with a mis-scoped span. A better answer-span instruction, shared by all three arms, would move more than the architecture choice being argued about.

The honest limitation. The workflow arm emits both retrieval queries from one decompose call, so hop 2 is written before hop 1 is read. That is a fixed DAG in its purest form, and it is weaker than a workflow a competent engineer would ship. Read “workflow” here as “cheapest possible fixed pipeline”, not “best fixed pipeline”.

What would change the answer. One fourth arm: retrieve(hop1) → extract bridge entity → retrieve(hop2). One more LLM call, genuine 2-hop capability, still fully deterministic and still a point mass on cost. It would almost certainly erase the loop's only non-significant advantages on HotpotQA — and it would still score the guessing floor on depth-4 chains, because the structural argument does not care how good the fixed pipeline is, only that it is fixed.

What this is not. n = 20 per cell with --repeats 1. Every point estimate here is noisy, which is precisely why the paired test is the thing reported and why only one result is claimed as a result. Variance between identical runs is one of the quantities being measured; a single pass cannot separate a real difference from sampling noise, and this page does not pretend otherwise.

DecisionModels: Kev-4B Against GPT-5.2, and What 32 Cells Can Actually Settle

Eight companies, four investment policies, the same 32 decisions put to kev-4b on a laptop, to gpt-5.2 through a gateway, and to a deterministic rule engine. The 81–88% agreement is mostly arithmetic: 20 cells are settled by a stated constraint, which the rule engine gets 20/20 for nothing, and always-AVOID alone scores 62%. On the 12 cells that need judgement the arms agree 58–67% — too few, and too split, to rank anyone. The separation that does hold: only the 4B model's confidence can be thresholded — 0.31 on judgement cells against 0.66 on breaches, where gpt-5.2 never writes a number below 0.62 — and only the 4B model stops re-deciding when the policy changes, 5 of 8 companies against 7 of 8. Rules for the arithmetic, a calibrated model for the escalation gate.

2The problem, and why it needs an experiment

In one sentence. Take a company's fundamentals as a document, take an investment policy that was written down before anyone looked at the company, and return one of three actions — BUY, HOLD or AVOID. Then put that identical decision to three different kinds of system, and see what separates them.

Three things that sentence hides, each of which changes how the numbers further down should be read:

  • AVOID is not sellThis is screening, not position management. Nothing here owns anything — there is no portfolio, no entry price and no SELL action in the harness at all. AVOID means “do not take a position under this policy”, which is exactly why one company can be a refusal and a buy in the same pass.
  • the unit is a pairA verdict belongs to a (company, policy) cell, never to a company. Eight companies against four policies is 32 decisions, and a system returning one answer for a company across all four policies has failed — however defensible that answer looks on its own. That is what the policy-sensitivity column in fold 6 measures.
  • not three modelsThree different kinds of thing, which is the whole reason the comparison is interesting: a scoring model handed the options, a generator asked to write one, and deterministic code that cannot be wrong about arithmetic. The rule engine is in the table to be agreed with, not to compete.

The problem. A large share of real enterprise work is one shape: a policy that someone wrote down, a case in front of you, and an action that has to be defensible against that policy rather than merely plausible. Credit decisions, underwriting, clinical triage, procurement thresholds, investment screening — the domain changes and the shape does not. Stock screening is the version used here because the policies can be written out in full and the arithmetic can be checked by hand, not because the interesting part is finance.

Why the obvious answer is unsatisfying. The instinct is to send it to the largest model available. What that buys is a text generator asked to also be a judge: it returns an action, and if you ask nicely, a confidence number it wrote. That number has no trained relationship to how often the model is right. It cannot be thresholded, which means the only operational question anyone actually has — which of these decisions should a human see? — has no answer. You either review everything or you review nothing.

What a decision model changes. A scoring model is a different shape: it is handed the options rather than asked to invent them, and it returns a probability from a trained head at a fitted temperature. “Escalate anything below p” becomes a line of code instead of a hope. That is the same System One argument the ToolSelection section above makes for a different stage of the pipeline, which is why it is worth testing on a problem where the right answer is sometimes objectively checkable.

So: two questions, and the second is the real one. First, is a 4B model running on a laptop actually behind a frontier model on these decisions? And if it is not meaningfully behind — which is the answer fold 6 reaches — then what property should decide which one gets deployed? An accuracy column that cannot separate them is not an argument for either.

And why the comparison had to be designed, not just run. Comparisons of this kind usually report one agreement number. That number silently mixes the cells where a stated constraint already settles the answer with the cells where a model is genuinely deciding, and the first kind flatters everyone in the table. Separating them is not a refinement of this experiment; it is the experiment. The floor in fold 5 was measured before any model was called, precisely so the headline could be checked against it.

3The difference, exactly — one cell, worked

A decision model is not a small chat model. Kev does not generate text and is not asked to. It takes the state — here, one company's fundamentals as a document — and scores supplied options in a single prefill-only pass, returning a calibrated probability over them. Each policy is a typed choice question over {BUY, HOLD, AVOID}, and the eight questions asked of one company share that company's prefill and cannot read each other.

The model never divides anything. Every ratio, every percentile and every bucket is computed in Python before the model is called, and what reaches it is a document of already-scored dimensions. That is deliberate: a measurement of policy application should not be contaminated by whether a 4B model can do arithmetic on net_debt_ebitda. It also means a failure here is a judgement failure, not a calculator failure.

HARD CONSTRAINTS (a breach forces AVOID regardless of other merits): LEVERAGE CEILING 2.5x; BETA CEILING 1.2; POSITIVE FCF REQUIRED. BUY - satisfies every hard constraint and fits the policy well on its weighted dimensions. HOLD - no hard constraint is breached, but the weighted evidence is mixed or marginal. AVOID - a hard constraint is breached, or the weighted evidence is clearly poor.

So the job is not financial reasoning. The ratios, the sector percentiles and the −2…+2 bucketing are all Python, and several lines arrive with the judgement already made in words — 48 (mid), 9.0 (fails), n/a (no EBITDA) elsewhere in the set. What is left is matching: one document, one policy with a thesis and five weights, three options with written criteria. Kev scores the three and returns a probability over them. A 4B model can do that without knowing what EBITDA is, which is the point — the task was shaped so that it would not have to.

The one thing it must work out for itself is a comparison, and that is exactly where it failed. Nothing tells either model which constraints are breached. The policy supplies a ceiling as a label, the document supplies a figure, and noticing that 2.6 exceeds 2.5 is the model's own work. Twenty of the thirty-two cells turn on precisely that, and Kev got nineteen. On this one it returned HOLD — and everything else in the document is a textbook capital-preservation holding: beta 0.4, a 6% drawdown, a 4.4% dividend, and market context is the joint-heaviest weight in the policy at 0.30. The weighted evidence is strongly favourable and one digit is not. It weighed the cell when it should have checked it, and the 0.24 confidence says it half knew.

The arena withholds the breach on purpose. It could have handed both arms the answer — the repository's offline verdict generator does exactly that, opening its prompt with PRE-CHECKED HARD CONSTRAINT BREACHES — and the arena deliberately does not, because a constraint a model is told about is not a constraint it has been tested on. The result is the argument for putting the comparison back into code in production. Twenty of these cells never needed a model at all; a rule engine gets every one of them right, for free, in no time. Spend the model on the twelve cells where there is nothing to compare.

5Design

Two kinds of cell, and only one of them is a test. Of the 32 company-policy cells, 20 breach a stated hard constraint. In those, AVOID is not an opinion — the policy says so in advance and the arithmetic is unambiguous, which makes them objective ground truth and makes getting them right unremarkable. The remaining 12 are judgement cells. Those are the only ones where a model can be interestingly right or wrong.

The floor, measured before any model ran. test_harness.py asks whether the fixtures could detect a bad model at all, by scoring three constant strategies against the rule engine. Always-AVOID scores 62%. Always-HOLD and always-BUY score 19% each. So 62% is the number any arm has to clear before its agreement means anything, and it is uncomfortably close to the headline results.

What the same harness says about the rule engine itself. Raising debt from 0 to 6× never improves a verdict under any of the four policies, so the engine is monotonic where it should be. Perturbing the policy weights by ±20% over 300 trials flips at most 0.9% of verdicts, so the weights are not load-bearing to the decimal. But 3 of the 12 judgement cells sit within 0.15 of a verdict boundary — which matters later, because a disagreement at a boundary is not the same finding as a disagreement in the middle of a range.

Agreement with the rule engine is not correctness. On the breach cells the engine is ground truth, because the policy wrote the constraint down. On the judgement cells it is just a fourth opinion — a weighted sum someone chose the weights for. An arm that disagrees with it there has not been shown to be wrong, and the column should be read as distance from a reference, never as a score.

7Insights, and what would change the answer
  • The benchmark mostly measures arithmetic. 20 of 32 cells are settled by a constraint written down in advance, and a constant AVOID scores 62%. An 81% headline is 19 points over a model that does not read anything. Report the judgement subset or report nothing.
  • Twelve cells cannot settle a comparison. The judgement-cell numbers — 58%, 67%, 67% — are three or four cells apart from each other. Nothing here distinguishes a 4B from a frontier model on policy application, and the honest claim is that the instrument is too small, not that the models are equal.
  • Some of the disagreement is boundary noise. 3 of the 12 judgement cells sit within 0.15 of a verdict boundary, and Kev's single hard-constraint miss was a 2.6 against a 2.5 ceiling. A cell that flips under a rounding error is not evidence about a model.
  • Calibration is the real difference, and it is not on the accuracy line. A trained probability that spans 0.10–0.95 and never contradicts the rules above 0.90 can carry a routing threshold. A self-reported number floored at 0.62 cannot, whatever its accuracy. If one of these arms goes into a system that escalates its uncertain calls, that is the column that decides, not agreement.
  • Where the 4B is actually behind is policy-following. It changes its verdict across the four policies for 5 of 8 companies against 7 of 8 for both other arms, and it names the same binding dimension as the rule engine only 18 of 32 times. It is reaching similar answers while reading the policy less closely — which is the failure that scales badly when the policies multiply.
  • What would change the answer. More judgement cells, by constructing fixtures that breach nothing — the set is currently 62% breach by accident of design. Real frozen fundamentals in place of archetypes, to test recall and application together rather than application alone. And a reference that is not the rule engine, because agreeing with a weighted sum is not the same as being right.
